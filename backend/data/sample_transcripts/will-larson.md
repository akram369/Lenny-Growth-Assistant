# The engineering mindset | Will Larson (Carta, Stripe, Uber, Calm, Digg)

## Transcript

Will Larson (00:00:00):
I think that we often treat engineers a little bit like children instead of giving them the responsibilities and ability to actually thrive as adults. And so like, "Oh, the engineers won't want to do that work." Well, that's actually not good for the engineers to be sheltered from what is important. And so actually one of the highlights is that I think we're coming back this moment where we can actually treat engineers like our peers and put them into really senior leadership roles and not have this kind of baseline assumption of like, "Oh, we have to coddle them or hide them from the real problems." And this is how they're going to get the opportunity to grow as well.

Lenny (00:02:15):
That's such a great framing. Will, when you look at engineering strategy versus product strategy, where do teams usually go wrong?

Will Larson (00:02:24):
Engineering strategy is simply the technical decisions that you make to support the business strategy. The most common mistake I see is writing a 40-page technical doc that nobody reads or cares about. A great strategy is just a sequence of decisions: what are we doing, what are we NOT doing, and why does this solve the customer problem? If engineers don't understand the customer or business metrics, they build complex architectures that collapse under maintenance weight.

Lenny (00:10:45):
How do you measure engineering velocity effectively without creating toxic incentives?

Will Larson (00:11:00):
You cannot measure individual lines of code or PR counts—that's a disaster. Instead, measure system cycle time: how long does it take an idea to go from a committed PR to running in production in front of customers? When deployment frequency is high and lead time is low, engineers are happy and the product moves rapidly. The four DORA metrics are the best baseline.

Lenny (00:24:30):
What advice do you give to a VP of Product working with a new CTO?

Will Larson (00:24:42):
Align on the non-functional requirements early. Product managers care about new feature surface area; engineers care about uptime, latency, and technical debt. If you don't explicitly allocate 20% of engineering bandwidth to foundational reliability, technical debt will eventually grind your product roadmap to a complete halt.
